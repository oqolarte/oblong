## Thoughts on *V for Vendetta*

I reread *V for Vendetta* because it was the 5th of November. The first
time was on my phone---not the medium the creators intended. This time,
I have a physical copy of the compilation.

David Lloyd's artwork of deep shadows gives a feeling of *verwirrung*.
The images ask me to attend to details that might have been lurking in
the dark by slowing down and not blitz through the entire graphic novel
in one sitting. Still, I find it hard to differentiate between
characters---the subtle facial expressions of British characters I
am not too familiar with.

The shadow work and the contrast in colors it forces on the readers
might be the artists' way of conveying the political factions at play.

In the monologue of fascist regime Norsefire's leader, Adam Susan, he
claims, "I believe in [fascism](/fascism)." This could have been written
better. Few would say that to themselves, because who thinks they are
the bad guy? Few would own a universally accepted bad term and somehow
be personally convinced that doing so is justified by their actions.

In the same chapter, the titular character, V, introduces the concept of
anarchy without explaining it beyond the fact that justice is
meaningless without freedom, *while* blowing up a statue. This
reinforces the stigma that anarchists act like terrorists. Is his
flamboyant need for explosions a worthy artistic choice (risk, really)
that might undermine his political message?

The novel also explores moral ambiguities. Should we outright dismiss
people when we find out that their [politics](/politics) are so
mismatched with ours?  Moore and Lloyd delve into some characters to
show us that what we think is villainy may not be so obvious for others.

The concept of hating another human being is much easier when one no
longer treats them as a fellow of equal rights. Delia Anne Surridge, a
medical researcher for the fascist regime, found it in her to hate her
subjects for the mere fact that they were deteriorating in captivity. To
many people like Delia, hate justifies torturous [violence](/violence)
against another person. Enough to use scientific method, no matter its
consequences, as a tool to alter lives. Before death flows painlessly in
her veins, she regrets what she had done for the regime.

Eric Finch is the lead investigator of the "Codename V", and Delia's
lover. To "crack this case" of finding out who V is and his motives for
the havoc he has caused, Finch wants to "think the way he [V] thinks."
Arguably, this means that Finch would have had an inkling of anarchist
beliefs. At that point, we must ask: "How transformational is it going
to be for him?"

Freedom has never been just a daydream, but it is fleeting because we
all share it. In the graphic novel, Finch is "set free" when he suffers
from a mental breakdown caused by a visit to a former concentration camp
site and some LSD use. In real life, such transformations---minus
mind-altering drugs---would have to happen to everyone everywhere all at
once. We must [*imagine*](/prefiguration) other paths forward.
Visual storytelling offers one way to do this collectively.

This is where graphic novels like *V for Vendetta* excel---they make the
imagination visible, giving readers a shared language to pull those
futures into the Here and Now.
