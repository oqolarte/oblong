## Thoughts on *Babel*

*Spoilers ahead*.

*Babel*[^title], a novel by R. F. Kuang, shows that academic work has affected
the real world. Some intellectuals and researchers from academia
might pretend that they are insulated from the outside world, but their
actions and inactions are always part of, and thus are affected by,
something bigger than them: from what gets funded to who will benefit
the transformational output of research, for better or worse.

[^title]: Full title: *Babel, or The Necessity of Violence: An Arcane
History of the Oxford Translators' Revolution*

*Babel* is the second dark academia novel that I have read. The first
was *The Revolution According to Raymundo Mata* by Gina Apostol, a
Filipina novelist. Apostol's novel also deals with
translation work. If one of the critiques against Kuang's *Babel* is her
use of footnotes, these people would surely despise Apostol's *Raymundo
Mata* because a huge chunk of the text is that: footnotes on footnotes,
all as literary device.  Personally, in both Kuang's and Apostol's work,
the use of footnotes in historical fiction is a creative choice I dig,
not as bad a distraction as some people believe.

When I started reading, I thought that maybe the text of *Babel* was too
dense, so much academic stuff by way of language and linguistics. I
thought that removing these, including the footnotes, would make the
book slimmer. But I realized that perhaps the
intellectual rambling on etymologies across multiple languages is part
of the experience. Kuang wants the readers to feel what it might be
like as a scholar.

Kuang asks, "Can powerful institutions be changed from within, or does
[revolution](/revolution) always require [violence](/violence)?"

The revolution, if it aspires [to win here and now and consistently](/prefiguration) into
the foreseeable future, must be based on trust, first and foremost.
I need to understand what it
is I am committing myself to. Otherwise, it is just blind faith, and I
am not religious enough for that. The platitudes of the Hermes Society,
an underground organization that resists the expansion of Britannia
in this novel, might inspire me to rebel against the injustices of the
[Empire](/empire), but until there is mutual trust between myself as a
potential newcomer and the Hermes Society, I will continue to have
doubts.

When one has seen so many deaths and been acquainted with such at
a young age, it becomes harder to believe that "Survival's not that
difficult." Living through multiple traumatic experiences could,
unsurprisingly, alter one's perception of what is and what is not a
dignified choice when wanting to live.

Contradictory truths occupying one's mind are like holding two phrases
of different languages, one supposedly a translation of the other. In
*Babel*, silver bars are inscribed with translations, and the meaning
lost is stored in the bar.[^silver] The silver bar holds the nuances
that vanish when one voyages from one language to another. Likewise,
holding multiple versions of a truth humming in the background affects
how one acts and feels and lives. Anything could release the energy.
What would be its effects---to the owner of these truths, to the
surrounding people?

Responding to Kuang's question, I posit another one: "Could activists
who support and do [direct action](/direct-action)---people who realize
that violence must not be monopolized by the Empire---ever work with
pacifists?" *Yes*. Top of mind, this might
include various self-defense and harm reduction strategies: for example,
protecting protesters from state violence, providing medical and
[mutual aid](/mutual-aid), creating escape routes.

A general strike, in theory and in practice, is hard to mount. Aside
from convincing the workers to put everything on hold and risk facing
brutality of the enforcers (state agents) that the state and
corporate power would inevitably send, the workers must also face the
ire of the public. In more pragmatic terms, "the public merely [wants]
all the conveniences of modern life without the guilt of knowing how
those conveniences were procured." Life is already hard for many of us
as it is; do we also have to put our livelihood on the line? Still, no
matter one's opinions about strikes, it remains one of the most powerful
nonviolent acts we can collectively do.

It is fine to romanticize the revolution. Let us not forget about the
daily practical arrangements that will make it work long-term. Every
day, we must answer this question: how do we eat, drink, bathe, use the
bathroom, sleep? If we cannot build and maintain the facilities that
enable basic necessities, we cannot supplant the current dominant
systems. To "shock the system" without care is to put innocent lives in
danger. And if your revolution involves harming people who did not
consent to receiving it, then it is not my revolution. What we want,
among other things, is to understand what the adversary is capable of
and prepare accordingly, so that when violence comes, we are not caught
unprepared, and we do not become what we fight against.

[^silver]: In the book, this is called *match-pair*, a pair of
translation between two languages. To invoke its effect, the speaker
must be fluent with both languages, before speaking the words, one after
the other. Here is my personal contribution to *Babel*: *gigil* is a
Filipino word for a strong feeling caused by anger, impatience,
yearning, thrill, or joy of seeing someone or something so adorable; its
pair, in English, could be *intense*. Invoking the *gigil-intense*
silver bar would grant its user heightened emotions and superhuman
strength, the latter of which is enough to crush bones. It must be used
carefully because while heightened, the user might not be able to think
straight and could cause irreversible or even fatal damage. The dual
nature of this proposed silver bar (cute rage, [joyful militancy](/joyful-militancy))
becomes more apparent further down my thoughts: the same
force the protects can also destroy, and preparation means knowing which
you are capable of when heightened.
