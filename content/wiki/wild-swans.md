## Intro

I think the author's intention is not write the history of 'China' as a
monolith, but to have an account of how diverse peoples, regions, and
traditions were forcibly unified under successive regimes---each
claiming to represent 'the people' while actually serving narrow
interests.

## A question of trust

The question of trust runs through all three generations.

The author's grandmother, Yu Fang, had asked an important question after
her daughter (the author's mother, Bao Qin) convinced her to donate most
of Yu Fang's jewelry to the Party. "Could one really trust the Communist
Party to look after everyone [f]orever?"

Extended across time and regimes, could one really trust any authority
from any political and economic system to govern millions of people for a
long time?

## Harmful Traditions and Women's Oppression

### Bound Feet and Institutionalized Exploitation

How did we even come up with horrible traditions that repeatedly inflict
pain on women's bodies, like "three-inch golden lilies" (bound feet)?
Can we not start any one more ever?

Powerful warlords in early 20th century China maintained rosters of
concubines---institutionalized mistresses, acquired and discarded at
will." This dynamic echoes the film *Raise the Red Lantern* (1991),
which portrayed relationships among a warlord's concubines as a
suffocating zero-sum game for the women involved.

### Learning from Harmful Traditions

In accepting radical changes to pave over traditions, we should
simultaneously learn how and why harmful traditions came about so that
we may unlearn them, not perpetuate their effects, and not start another
one that is just as similar. Old monsters cannot transform to
accommodate new ones. For example, we must ditch the opulence of
weddings but we have every right to maintain the joy of celebrating the
union of love between two (or more, if we ever come to that point)
people.

## The Nature of Colonial and Authoritarian Violence

### Colonial Domination

I view colonizers as necessarily evil. Theirs has been a project of dominating
entire ecosystems, including dehumanized people, for the colonizer's
benefit. Evil manifests itself in many forms of violence, not the least
of which is causing agony and death.

### Authoritarian Tactics: Drawing Lines Between "In" and "Out"

Authoritarians, such as the Communist Party in China in 1949, were keen
to isolate, to "draw the line" between those who were "in" and those who
were "out." This defining of large-scale tribalism is one of the first
steps to make an enemy of "other" people.

### Violence and Revolution

Violence in revolution is inescapable. Those in power will use violent
forces to keep the status quo. Revolutionaries fighting the long fight
for justice and equity for all should then be careful with human lives.
Violence as self-defense is necessary, but not executions.

A so-called revolution that locks up its revolutionaries (ideologically
or otherwise), prevents them from venturing out of the system, and does
not allow people the human act of crying to express whatever intense
feelings they might have, is not my revolution.

## Mao's Political Campaigns: Pattern of Purges

### The Rectification Campaign (1942)

When Mao Zedong started a "Rectification" campaign in Yan'an in 1942,
was the end goal really to rectify how things were run, or to purge
critics from the Party?

### The Anti-Rightist Campaign (1957)

In spring of 1957, some 15 years after the so-called Rectification
campaign in Yan'an, Mao and the Party "urged the intellectuals to
criticize officials all the way to the top." Consistent with Mao's
legacy, this would be another witch hunt to do away with impure
Communist thought. If it is not ridiculous enough, there even was a
quota: "5% of the intellectuals were rightists."

Then, as it is now, as it will always be, assigning a quota to be met
will never produce net positive results, especially in political
campaigns that directly affect everyday lives. To implement a quota in
any part of revolution is just a rebranding of old ways.

### The Cultural Revolution (1966)

Mao officially launched the Cultural Revolution in 1966 to start yet
again another political cleansing against Party officials who were not
loyal to him and thus deemed as those "following the capitalist road."

Liu Shaoqi, then the president of Communist China, was instrumental in
the deification of Mao. Ironically, though, he was branded as "China's
Khrushchev," a derogatory label at the time. Since Liu had opposed Mao,
Liu was believed to be orchestrating Mao's downfall. Mao then targeted
Liu in his propaganda.

## The Great Leap Forward and Famine

### Industrial Quotas Without Infrastructure

Another one of Mao's quotas was the doubling of steel production from
5.35 million tons in 1957 to 10.7 million in 1958 without properly
expanding China's steel industry. Instead, the quota must be met by the
entire population. Millions and millions of farms had to move from
agricultural work to steel production to meet this quota.

This industrialization is dubbed Great Leap Forward, and the famine that
followed right after this suggests that the direction to which the
Chinese population was made to leap forward was towards death. This is
what happens when absolute power meets absolute ignorance, resulting in
millions of deaths, including destruction of ecosystems.

### The Unmentionable Famine

The Great Famine (sometimes called the Great Leap Forward, depending on
who is talking) was "officially unmentionable" under Mao's rule. Reality
and un-reality were both shaping the lives of Chinese people during this
time. Imagine a doctor being condemned as a class enemy simply because
he made a truthful diagnosis of hunger victims. Science, whether in a
capitalist society or in an authoritarian communist regime, is never
just about an objective truth. It is affected by the people in power.

## Control of Thought and Culture

### Suppressing Independent Thinking

Mao did not want independent thinking, which Mao thought "might lead to
less than total obedience to him." Authoritarian regimes have never been
progressive, because it does not seek total liberation for all. Instead,
it uses the illusion of progressivism to lure people who want a decent
life, before saying, "Surprise! We the Party still want dominion over
all of you."

### The Personal Is Political---But Whose Politics?

There is/was another interpretation of the line "The personal is
political" in the Communist Party: to dedicate one's life and to submit
it fully to the Central Party's political endeavors in advancing its
revolution. Revolution was to be prioritized, as if love in
personal lives is not revolutionary.

### Controlling Culture to Control Thinking

Throughout 1967 and 1968 in Mao's Communist China, there was barely
anything to do for the common folk. To control culture, and thus
thinking, the following were outlawed: books, films, music, theater,
museums, and teahouses.

Chanting "Long Live Chairman Mao" mechanically at night was dystopic
levels of authoritarianism. This reminds me of George Orwell's *1984*.
Orwell was likely inspired by another dystopia of his time, but who
would have thought that Mao would get parts from it, knowingly or
otherwise, and think that it was a blueprint, not a warning, for a
society he had intended to bend?

Kudos to the Revolutionary Committee (circa 1969) for innovating
"[g]rotesque forms of worshiping Mao," one of which was completing
sentences of one another as a form of greeting. The sentences were often
excerpts of Mao Zedong Thought.

### The Cult of Mao

When Mao convinced himself to be essentially the emperor of Communist
China, his followers, then and now, have convinced themselves to revere
him. Power corrupts absolutely. Communism and socialism, if these had
any chance at succeeding, should never nosedive to personality cults.

The cult of Mao was like any other cult that had some form of success in
controlling its followers: "by fear and indoctrination that to deviate
from the path laid down […] would have been inconceivable." Cults, like
Mao's, also overwhelmed its people "by deceptive rhetoric,
disinformation, and hypocrisy, which made it virtually impossible to see
through the situation and to form intelligent judgment."

### Anti-Intellectualism

Anti-intellectualism was also rampant during the Cultural Revolution.
Mao hated education, and he let the closure and disintegration of
universities. He also hated intellectuals; they had been the targets of
previous Party purges (see the section [Anti-Rightist Campaign, 1957](#the-anti-rightist-campaign-1957)).
It is always harder to control learned
people than less educated people. So, ignorance was encouraged, celebrated
even, and intelligence was shunned.

It has always been important for teachers to teach critical thinking,
especially in political matters affecting our daily lives. But I highly
doubt this includes imparting state propaganda to students.

### Barefoot Doctors and the Peasantry

Mao's Communist Party had always elevated the peasants as the models to
emulate. In the Party's view, the peasants "were the most unquestioning
and submissive."

Barefoot doctors were doctors who lived with the peasants. These doctors
supposedly refused to wear a pair of shoes (perhaps their only good
pair) to the muddy fields. More importantly, barefoot doctors did not
have proper medical training.

The author Jung Chang describes the text in propaganda newspapers as
"overblown, vapid language."

## Survival Under Authoritarianism

### The Cost of Honesty

During Mao's rule, there was no place for honest and honorable people.
To survive the regime was to know enough of the lies to start spewing
and eventually believing them, or to be actually so ignorant that there
could be no other way of living except believing whatever was force-fed
to you. Honesty led to destruction, and the process left no dignity
intact.

### A Personal Case: Chang Shou-yu

Chang Shou-yu, the author's father, a high official in the Party, was
arrested and detained for trying to speak up against the appointment of
Ting family in a high position in Chengdu Province. The abuse he got during
his detainment led to the development of schizophrenia. When Chang
Shou-yu was released, it took some time before he was admitted to a
mental hospital where he received electric shocks and insulin shots.

### Torture and dehumanization

Torture is an evil way to destroy one's opponents. It is scary to think
that given the right environment, many of us are capable of doing that
to another human. Such environment might foster unfathomable hate for
some "other", and the facilitator would be a cult leader like Chairman
Mao.


## Parallels and Comparisons

### Philippine Context: NatDem and Duterte

When I read stories about how people in Mao's China had used the
reckless violence of Red Guards to settle old scores with their
neighbors, I am reminded by the state agents, many of whom are/were from
Philippine National Police, who enacted extrajudicial killings during
Duterte's Operation Tokhang. In both cases, the lack of due process led
to the destruction of innocent lives. This gap in the justice system was
then weaponized by vindictive people. Both cases show that the state
will try to preserve its power by monopolizing violence to destroy some
perceived "other" enemy.

As a Filipino, I find it hard to discuss Mao's Communist Party without
addressing the Philippine National Democracy (NatDem). NatDem's politics
is highly derived from Marxism-Leninism-Maoism.  Like in Mao's China,
there is a separate mass of people to which NatDem believers must
dedicate their actions, possibly missing the point that unless they are
scions (and thus benefactors) of the current ruling class, they too are
part of the masses. I imagine that in a more egalitarian society,
everybody treats everybody equally and with equity, no longer requiring
to put some people on a pedestal.

When Joma Sison adopted Mao's teachings and localized them for the
Communist Party of the Philippines, it raises the question, "Did he also
absorb the megalomania of Mao?"

Also, NatDem initially endorsed the presidential
candidacy of Duterte in 2016. The two camps would later have a falling out,
leading Duterte to eventually mark the NatDem as terrorists.

### A Personal Anecdote: Desperation and Hunger

When I was in high school, one of our teachers had the whole class
participating in an activity of improvisational storytelling.
As the story would [d]evolve, it became a little harder for the next
student to think of a "plot progression" that would flow nicely.

I had forgotten most of the story, even my contribution to it,
but it was about a poverty-stricken family, who had to bury (or maybe
compost) a pile of rotting fish. Bob (not his real name), known for his
unpredictable creativity, was up. He added (in Tagalog), "But the boy
was so hungry he dug up the fish and started eating them." In improv
comedy, it would have been funny, as it was at the time. But the teacher
went livid. She said, "Who in their right minds would do such thing?!"
Bob had to revise his contribution into a more humane one.

Now that I am reading this, such acts of desperation---of digging up
rotten food from the earth with the explicit notion of trying to eat
it---is within the realm of possibility after all! At least it was in
China under Kuomintang regime. And perhaps even more stories like this
from the Great Famine of Communist China remain untold to this day.

## Slogans and Contradictions

### "Serve the People" and "Never Forget Class Struggle"

Two of Mao's slogans had been "Serve the people" and "Never forget class
struggle." I agree. I am part of the people, so it is my right that I
also do good things for myself as well as for others. In fact, I would
go so far as to say both of them simply expanded on what Jesus had said
almost two thousand years before: "Love your neighbor as yourself" (in
the Christian bible).

### Rigid systems break

Rigid things break spectacularly and inevitably at some point in the
future. Mao's empire was no different. Until then, would it have been
worth all the trouble to be rigid, in this case defending Mao's brand of
Communism, especially at the cost of important relationships?

Just like corporate capitalism, Mao's Communist China did not want
people to have free time. If not working, people must be attending
meetings where each should self-criticize. Unfortunately, pettiness
dominated these meetings, leading one to ask whether they were effective
at "purifying" one's thoughts at all.

## On Maoism

What if Mao's "philosophy" can be distilled to be that of promoting
fights to satiate his need for perpetual conflict? This suggests that
hiding beneath all the layers of appropriated socialism, Maoism seeks to
sow discord.

## Final thoughts

"The priority in my life," the author had said to her father one time,
"is to have fun." I admire this level of candor for one's own fate, even
though such fate cannot be isolated from the others. And so, while it is
more than okay to have that kind of priority, we must also ask, "Up to
what extent should I have fun? Should I alone have all the fun, or can I
perhaps multiply it by sharing it with others? When the fun is being
had, how does it affect other people, other non-human beings, and the
environment, both near and far?" We ask these questions because everyone
deserves to have fun.

Humor has always been powerful as it has the ability to chip away at the
facade of any oppressive empire. Court jesters may have been allowed to
poke fun at the regime, but they still must work within the confines
designated by their overlords. The real fun of joking around lies in the
banter between close people.
